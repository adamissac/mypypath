Unit 4 • Lesson 1

The Series

Overview

A column with labels attached, and the labels travel with the data.

Intermediate 20–25 min
← Previous Next: Building a DataFrame

What You Will Learn in This Lesson

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Build a Series with an index and read a value by its label.
  • Say what a Series has that a numpy array does not.
  • Use the numpy operations you already know on a Series.
  • Tell max from idxmax, and know which question each answers.

Why This Matters

A DataFrame is a collection of Series, so everything here is the foundation for the next six units. The index in particular is not decoration: it is what survives a filter, what lines two columns up when you add them, and what turns "the highest score" into "who got the highest score".

Step 1: A labelled column

A Series is one column of values plus an index. Build one from a list and pandas supplies 0, 1, 2 as the index; supply your own and the labels become how you reach the values.

1

Values and labels together

The index is printed down the left. Reaching for a label gives you the value beside it, in the same way a dictionary key would.

import pandas as pd

scores = pd.Series([92, 88, 79], index=["Ada", "Grace", "Alan"])
print(scores)
print(scores["Grace"])
2

The labels survive what you do to it

This is the part that matters. Filter the Series and the surviving values keep their own labels, so you never have to track which position belonged to whom.

import pandas as pd

scores = pd.Series([92, 88, 79], index=["Ada", "Grace", "Alan"])
high = scores[scores > 85]
print(high)
print(list(high.index))

Step 2: Everything numpy did, a Series does too

Masks, arithmetic and aggregation all carry over unchanged. What is added is that the labels come with them.

1

The familiar operations

If unit 3 made sense, none of this is new. A Series is an array with an index bolted on.

import pandas as pd

scores = pd.Series([92, 88, 79], index=["Ada", "Grace", "Alan"])
print(scores.mean())
print(scores + 5)
print(scores[scores > 85])
2

Two different questions

max gives the value. idxmax gives the label of the row it was on. Confusing them is easy and the wrong one is never an error, just a wrong answer.

import pandas as pd

scores = pd.Series([92, 88, 79], index=["Ada", "Grace", "Alan"])
print(scores.max())      # what the best score was
print(scores.idxmax())   # who got it
3

Alignment, and where NaN comes from

Adding two Series matches them on their labels, not their positions. A label present in one and missing from the other gives NaN — which is either exactly what you wanted or the quietest bug in pandas.

import pandas as pd

term1 = pd.Series([90, 80], index=["Ada", "Grace"])
term2 = pd.Series([70, 60], index=["Grace", "Alan"])
print(term1 + term2)

Nothing is out of order and nothing raised. Ada and Alan are NaN because each appears in only one of the two terms.

Step 3: When to Use It

One labelled column

Values with meaningful labels — scores by name, sales by month — where lookups use the label.

scores = pd.Series([92, 88], index=["Ada", "Grace"])

Arithmetic that should align

Two Series combine by label, so values match up even when the order differs.

When Not to Use It

Do not assume two Series line up by position. They align by label, and a label missing from one side produces NaN rather than an error.

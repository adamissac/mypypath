Labels and Honesty

Overview

A chart without units is a decoration, and a cut axis is an argument.

Intermediate 20–25 min
← Previous Next: Formatting Numbers

What You Will Learn in This Lesson

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Say what every chart needs before it is readable.
  • Explain what a truncated axis does to a comparison.
  • State a comparison in words, with its size.
  • Recognise a choice that is making an argument for you.

Why This Matters

Every chart makes a claim. The labels are what let a reader check it, and the axis is what decides how big the claim looks. Neither is a styling question: a chart with no units and a zoomed axis can make a one per cent difference look like a crisis, without a single number being wrong.

Step 1: The label is part of the answer

A number with no unit is not information. The reader cannot tell pounds from thousands of pounds from a percentage.

1

What a chart needs

A title saying what it shows, both axes labelled with units, and a note of how many observations are behind it. Four things, and a chart missing any of them is asking the reader to guess.

# title:  "Mean exam score by team, 2024"
# y axis: "Mean score (out of 100)"
# x axis: "Team"
# note:   "n = 48 students"
print("the units are half the claim")
2

The claim in words

If you cannot write the sentence, the chart is not finished. The sentence needs the direction, the size and the comparison.

import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({"team": ["red", "red", "blue"], "score": [90.0, 80.0, 70.0]})
means = df.groupby("team")["score"].mean().round(1)
gap = round(float(means.max() - means.min()), 1)
print(f"{means.idxmax()} leads on {means.max()}, {gap} ahead of {means.idxmin()}")

Step 2: A truncated axis exaggerates

Starting a bar chart's axis at 80 rather than 0 makes a small difference fill the frame. It can be defensible; it is never neutral.

1

The same numbers, two impressions

85 and 82 differ by three and a half per cent. On an axis from 80 to 90 one bar is more than twice the other.

import pandas as pd

means = pd.Series({"red": 85.0, "blue": 82.0})
print(means)
print("real difference:", round(float(means.max() - means.min()), 1))
print("as a share of the smaller:", f"{(means.max() - means.min()) / means.min():.1%}")

full = means / 100
zoomed = (means - 80) / 10
print("bar heights on a 0-100 axis:", full.round(2).to_dict())
print("bar heights on an 80-90 axis:", zoomed.round(2).to_dict())

The numbers did not change. The picture did. If you truncate an axis, say so on the chart — and be sure the reason is that the detail matters, not that the difference looked small.

2

The other quiet arguments

A colour scale with an off-centre midpoint, a y-axis that is a rate in one panel and a total in the next, a time axis with uneven gaps. Each is a decision the reader will not see you make.

import pandas as pd

# Totals favour the big region; rates favour the small one.
df = pd.DataFrame({"region": ["big", "small"], "sales": [1000.0, 300.0], "people": [500, 50]})
df["per_person"] = df["sales"] / df["people"]
print(df)
print("by total:", df.loc[df["sales"].idxmax(), "region"])
print("by rate: ", df.loc[df["per_person"].idxmax(), "region"])

Step 3: When to Use It

Every chart you share

A title that states the finding, axis labels with units, and the data source.

Small differences that matter

A cut axis is acceptable when it is labelled clearly and the difference is the point.

When Not to Use It

Do not start a bar chart’s axis above zero. Bar length is the value, and a cut axis turns a 2% gap into what looks like double.
